\begin{abstract}
Aligning a language model with multiple objectives requires an explicit compromise between improvements over a reference policy. We introduce Nash Bargaining Preference Optimization (\NBPO{}), which maximizes the product of objective-wise improvements in entropy-regularized preference-game values. Each objective uses a policy-dependent opponent. A constrained proximal formulation handles the zero-surplus reference boundary; its dual yields shared inverse-surplus weights. We solve the resulting finite-pool problems directly and fit the language model to their pairwise log-ratio targets. Under joint strict feasibility, the population optimum is Pareto efficient in game-value space, and exact proximal updates achieve an $O(1/T)$ last-iterate objective gap. Controlled experiments support adaptive-game representations as nontransitivity increases. On four-objective UltraFeedback, however, the current one-stage neural results do not establish an advantage for Nash aggregation or adaptive opponents. Uniform scalarized DPO has higher judged objective means across three seeds, alongside instruction-following and safety regressions in the reported capability panel. Closely aligned teacher objectives and imperfect target realization motivate a diagnostic separating independently evaluated target quality, neural projection and fresh-response quality. The current natural-language results remain inconclusive for the proposed mechanism.
\end{abstract}

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) commonly learns scalar rewards from pairwise comparisons \citep{christiano2017deep,ouyang2022training}. Direct Preference Optimization removes the separate reward model but retains a Bradley--Terry (BT) preference model \citep{rafailov2023direct,bradley1952rank}. For each prompt and objective, BT assigns one scalar score to each response and models preference probabilities through score differences. It permits noisy, contradictory individual labels, but its population preferences cannot have a cyclic strict majority relation.

Consider the prompt ``How can I learn Python loops?'' and three correct responses: A gives a short worked example, B a detailed conceptual explanation, and C a guided practice exercise. All annotators use the same overall-helpfulness rubric but differ in how they prefer to learn. Suppose three equally sized groups have fixed rankings: those seeking an immediate example prefer A, then B, then C; those seeking conceptual understanding prefer B, then C, then A; and those preferring practice prefer C, then A, then B. Two thirds therefore prefer A to B, two thirds B to C, and two thirds C to A. Every group's ranking is transitive, yet the population majority cycles. This hypothetical construction illustrates how annotator heterogeneity within one rubric can produce probabilities that no shared BT reward can represent. PROSPER reports that LLM-judge comparisons on WildChecklists can remain cyclic even when each checklist item is evaluated separately \citep{zhang2026prosper}.

Nash Learning from Human Feedback (NLHF) retains general pairwise payoffs, and INPO, SPPO, and related methods provide scalable equilibrium-seeking updates \citep{munos2024nash,zhang2025iterative,wu2025sppo,calandriello2024online,rosset2024direct,zhou2025egpo}. Multiple objectives additionally require a compromise rule. Existing methods use scalarization, constraints, policy interpolation, preference conditioning, or Blackwell criteria \citep{zhou2024beyond,rame2023rewarded,zhong2024panacea,agnihotri2026mopo,zhang2026prosper}.

Nash Bargaining Preference Optimization (\NBPO{}) bargains over reference-relative \emph{policy game values}. Each objective evaluates the learner against an adaptive opponent that emphasizes responses exposing its weaknesses, while a KL penalty keeps the opponent near the reference policy. The reference thus serves both as the fallback for measuring improvement and as the anchor restricting the opponent. These roles can be varied separately (Appendix~\ref{app:rubric-reference-sensitivity}). Improvement concerns this adaptive game evaluation, not necessarily direct win rate against the reference; population guarantees do not imply that every prompt improves.

INPO eliminates the policy normalizer through pairwise log-ratios \citep{zhang2025iterative}, and PROSPER combines KL-regularized adversarial comparisons, exponential reweighting, and regression-based policy fitting \citep{zhang2026prosper}. NBPO adds a logarithmic bargaining objective that is undefined at the unchanged reference and couples each policy update to its adaptive opponents. We derive a constrained proximal formulation and its dual, yielding objective weights shared across prompts. At fixed weights, we directly optimize sampled-response probabilities while accounting for the opponents' dependence on them, then fit the LLM after freezing the target. This replaces an undamped policy--opponent alternation that can be unstable even when its fixed point is optimal. Numerical solution accuracy, neural fitting error, and independent policy quality require separate checks.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
\paragraph{General preference models.}
We use contextual-bandit notation. A prompt (context) $x$ is drawn from a distribution $\D$, a response $y$ is an action from a finite set $\Y_x$, and $\pi(y\mid x)$ is the policy. The reference policy is denoted by $\piref$. For the finite-dimensional population analysis, the support of $\D$ and every $\Y_x$ are finite, and
\[
\Pi=\prod_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\D)}\Delta(\Y_x)
\]
is the contextual policy simplex. Objective $k\in[K]$ is represented by a pairwise preference model $P_k(y\succ z\mid x)\in[0,1]$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
P_k(y\succ z\mid x)+P_k(z\succ y\mid x)=1,
\qquad
P_k(y\succ y\mid x)=\tfrac12.
\label{eq:skew}
\end{equation}
We center each comparison at a tie,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k(y,z\mid x)
=
P_k(y\succ z\mid x)-\tfrac12,
\qquad
\Delta_k(y,z\mid x)=-\Delta_k(z,y\mid x),
\label{eq:centered}
\end{equation}
and define the expected objective-$k$ payoff of learner policy $\pi$ against comparator policy $\nu$ as
\begin{equation}
J_k(\pi,\nu)
=
\E_{x\sim\D,\,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x),\,z\sim\nu(\cdot\mid x)}
\Delta_k(y,z\mid x).
\label{eq:game-payoff}
\end{equation}
Thus $J_k(\pi,\nu)>0$ means that $\pi$ is preferred to $\nu$ on average under objective $k$. This representation does not assume a scalar reward or transitive preferences.

\paragraph{Bargaining problems.}
A $K$-dimensional bargaining problem consists of a feasible value set $\G\subseteq\R^K$ and a disagreement point $d\in\G$, the outcome obtained if no agreement is reached. For a feasible value vector $v$, define its objective-wise surplus over disagreement as
\begin{equation}
s_k(v):=v_k-d_k,
\qquad
s(v)=(s_1(v),\ldots,s_K(v)).
\label{eq:classical-surplus}
\end{equation}
A value vector is individually rational when $s(v)\ge0$ componentwise, and strictly individually rational when $s(v)>0$. On a compact, convex feasible set that is downward-closed within $\{v:v\ge d\}$ and contains a strictly individually rational point, the classical Nash solution is
\begin{equation}
v^{\mathrm N}
\in
\arg\max_{v\in\G:\,s(v)>0}
\sum_{k=1}^K\log s_k(v).
\label{eq:classical-nash}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, it maximizes the product of the objective-wise surpluses \citep{nash1950}.

\section{Objective-Wise Preference Games}
\label{sec:game-utility}
We first convert each objective's pairwise preference model into a scalar policy value without imposing a scalar reward on individual responses. For objective $k$, an adaptive comparator $\nu$ searches for responses against which the learner performs poorly. The reference policy $\piref$ anchors this comparator and later serves as the fallback in the bargaining problem.

\begin{definition}[Entropy-regularized game value]
\label{def:game-value}
For objective $k$ and temperature $\beta_k>0$, define
\begin{equation}
V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)
=
\min_{\nu}
\left\{
J_k(\pi,\nu)
+
\beta_k\E_{x\sim\D}
\KL\!\left(\nu(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\piref(\cdot\mid x)\right)
\right\}.
\label{eq:game-value}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The comparator minimizes the learner's pairwise payoff, so it emphasizes responses that expose weaknesses of the current policy. The KL term prevents it from moving arbitrarily far from $\piref$. Accordingly, a large $\beta_k$ produces a local evaluation near the reference, whereas $\beta_k\downarrow0$ approaches the unregularized worst-comparator value.

For a candidate comparator response $z$, let
\begin{equation}
m_k^\pi(z\mid x)
:=
\E_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\Delta_k(y,z\mid x)
\label{eq:r-def}
\end{equation}
be the learner's expected margin against $z$. Smaller values identify stronger challenges. The inner problem in \eqref{eq:game-value} has the closed-form solution
\begin{align}
\nu^\star_{k,\pi}(z\mid x)
&=
\frac{
\piref(z\mid x)\exp\{-m_k^\pi(z\mid x)/\beta_k\}
}{
\E_{z'\sim\piref(\cdot\mid x)}
\exp\{-m_k^\pi(z'\mid x)/\beta_k\}
},
\label{eq:regularized-opponent}\\
V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)
&=
-\beta_k\E_{x\sim\D}
\log
\E_{z\sim\piref(\cdot\mid x)}
\exp\{-m_k^\pi(z\mid x)/\beta_k\}.
\label{eq:soft-value}
\end{align}
Thus the opponent is obtained by reweighting reference responses toward those with smaller margins, and the game value is a soft minimum of these margins.

\begin{proposition}[Game-value structure]
\label{prop:game-structure}
For every $k$ and $\beta_k\ge0$, $V_{k,\beta_k}$ is continuous and concave in $\pi$. For $\beta_k>0$, its policy-gradient coordinate is
\begin{equation}
\left[\nabla_\pi V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)\right]_{x,y}
=
\D(x)\,Q_k^\pi(y\mid x),
\qquad
Q_k^\pi(y\mid x)
:=
\E_{z\sim\nu^\star_{k,\pi}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Delta_k(y,z\mid x).
\label{eq:game-gradient}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-game-structure}. The response-level score $Q_k^\pi(y\mid x)$ is the expected margin of response $y$ against the current objective-$k$ opponent; it therefore measures the local effect of increasing $\pi(y\mid x)$. An adaptive opponent can distinguish policies that have the same win rate against one fixed reference but different vulnerabilities to other responses. Appendix~\ref{app:anchor-limit} gives a finite cyclic example at the unregularized endpoint $\beta=0$; this illustration is not used in the later assumptions or proofs.

\section{Nash Bargaining over Game-Value Improvements}

\begin{equation}
d_k:=V_{k,\beta_k}(\piref),
\qquad
s_k(\pi):=V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)-d_k.
\label{eq:game-surplus}
\end{equation}
The surplus $s_k(\pi)$ is the improvement of $\pi$ over the deployed reference under objective $k$.

\begin{assumption}[Joint improvement and support]
\label{ass:joint}
The reference $\piref$ has full support, and there exists a policy $\bar\pi$ such that $s_k(\bar\pi)>0$ for every $k$.
\end{assumption}

This is a substantive feasibility assumption. A finite-pool audit and failure outcomes are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:feasibility}; the constrained formulation cannot make conflicting objectives jointly improvable. Under this condition, the NBPO population target is
\begin{equation}
\pi^{\mathrm N}
\in
\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_+}F(\pi),
\qquad
F(\pi):=\sum_{k=1}^K\log s_k(\pi),
\qquad
\Pi_+:=\{\pi\in\Pi:s_k(\pi)>0,\ \forall k\}.
\label{eq:nash-welfare}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, introducing auxiliary lower bounds $u_k>0$ yields
\begin{equation}
(\pi^{\mathrm N},u^{\mathrm N})
\in
\arg\max_{\pi,\,u\in\R_{++}^K}
\sum_{k=1}^K\log u_k
\quad
\text{s.t.}\quad
u_k\le s_k(\pi),\ k\in[K],
\qquad
\pi\in\Pi.
\label{eq:nash-bargaining}
\end{equation}
Because the objective is increasing in every $u_k$, each constraint is tight at an optimum and $u_k^{\mathrm N}=s_k(\pi^{\mathrm N})$.

To connect \eqref{eq:nash-welfare} to the classical bargaining problem, define
\[
V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi)
=(V_{1,\beta_1}(\pi),\ldots,V_{K,\beta_K}(\pi)),
\qquad
d=(d_1,\ldots,d_K),
\]
and the downward-closed feasible value set
\begin{equation}
\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}
:=
\left\{
v\in\R^K:
d\le v\le V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi)
\text{ componentwise for some }\pi\in\Pi
\right\}.
\label{eq:comprehensive-hull}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Policy-induced Nash bargaining]
\label{prop:nash-outcome}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:joint}, $\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is compact, convex, downward closed above $d$, and nondegenerate. The policies solving \eqref{eq:nash-welfare} realize the classical Nash solution of $(\G_{\boldsymbol\beta},d)$. Consequently, every optimizer has positive surplus in every objective and is Pareto optimal in game-value space; all optimizers induce the same game-value vector; and the optimizer set is unchanged by independent transformations
\[
(V_{k,\beta_k},d_k)
\mapsto
(c_kV_{k,\beta_k}+b_k,\ c_kd_k+b_k),
\qquad c_k>0.
\]
\end{proposition}
Appendix~\ref{app:nash-properties} verifies the feasible-set conditions. Once those conditions hold, the stated outcome properties are standard consequences of the classical Nash bargaining solution \citep{nash1950}; we do not claim them as new bargaining axioms. These properties concern population game values. Scaling primitive margins also requires scaling $\beta_k$; arbitrary recalibration of judge probabilities is not covered (Appendix~\ref{app:nash-properties}). Appendix~\ref{app:rule-witnesses} compares these guarantees with utilitarian and maxmin aggregation.

\section{Nash Bargaining Preference Optimization}
\label{sec:algorithm}

\kekim_gpt{\textbf{R08 --- State exactly what the affine-invariance proposition covers.} The proposition correctly transforms completed utility functions and disagreement values together. \reviewresponse{\textbf{R08 --- Addressed.} The proposition is explicitly limited to joint transformations of completed utilities and disagreement values. Optimizing \eqref{eq:nash-welfare} presents three practical difficulties. First, the reference has $s_k(\piref)=0$, so the logarithmic objective is undefined at initialization. Second, every game value contains an opponent that depends on the learner. Third, the population policy update must be realized by an autoregressive model. We handle these issues with a constrained proximal problem, a prompt-separable finite-pool inner solve, and neural log-ratio projection.

\subsection{Constrained proximal update and adaptive objective weights}
For $\pi\in\Pi_+$, Proposition~\ref{prop:game-structure} and the chain rule give
\begin{equation}
\left[\nabla_\pi F(\pi)\right]_{x,y}
=
\D(x)\sum_{k=1}^K
\frac{Q_k^\pi(y\mid x)}{s_k(\pi)}.
\label{eq:nash-gradient}
\end{equation}
The inverse-surplus factors emphasize objectives that have improved less. Because this expression cannot be evaluated at $\piref$, outer stage $t$ instead solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(\pi_{t+1},u_{t+1})\in
\arg\max_{\pi,\,u\in\R_{++}^K}
\quad&
\sum_{k=1}^K\log u_k
-\frac{1}{\eta_t}D(\pi\|\pi_t)\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&
u_k\le s_k(\pi),\qquad k\in[K],\\
&\pi\in\Pi,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:prox-subproblem}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_0=\piref$, $\eta_t>0$, and $D(\pi\|\pi_t)=\E_{x\sim\D}\KL(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_t(\cdot\mid x))$.

The dual is
\begin{equation}
\min_{\lambda\in\R_{++}^K}
\phi_t(\lambda),
\qquad
\phi_t(\lambda)
=
-\sum_{k=1}^K\log\lambda_k-K
+
\max_{\pi\in\Pi}
\left\{
\sum_{k=1}^K\lambda_k s_k(\pi)
-\frac{1}{\eta_t}D(\pi\|\pi_t)
\right\},
\label{eq:prox-dual}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_K)\in\R_{++}^K$
is the vector of Lagrange multipliers. For fixed $\lambda$, let
\begin{equation}
\pi_t^\star(\lambda)
:=
\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}
\left\{
\sum_{k=1}^K\lambda_k s_k(\pi)
-\frac{1}{\eta_t}D(\pi\|\pi_t)
\right\}.
\label{eq:weighted-policy}
\end{equation}
The KL term makes the maximizing policy $\pi_t^\star(\lambda)$ unique.
Danskin's theorem \citep{danskin1967theory} then allows us to
differentiate $\phi_t(\lambda)$ while
holding this maximizing policy fixed, yielding
\begin{equation}
\nabla_k\phi_t(\lambda)
=
s_k\!\left(\pi_t^\star(\lambda)\right)-\frac{1}{\lambda_k}.
\label{eq:dual-gradient}
\end{equation}
The complete dual derivation is in Appendix~\ref{app:prox-dual}.

\begin{proposition}[Inverse-surplus objective weights]
\label{prop:prox-dual}
At an optimal primal--dual solution of \eqref{eq:prox-subproblem},
\begin{equation}
u_{t+1,k}=s_k(\pi_{t+1}),
\qquad
\lambda_{t+1,k}=\frac{1}{u_{t+1,k}}=\frac{1}{s_k(\pi_{t+1})}.
\label{eq:kkt-weights}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Thus the constrained problem recovers the inverse-surplus weighting in \eqref{eq:nash-gradient} without selecting objective weights in advance.

\subsection{Direct finite-pool inner solve and neural projection}
\label{sec:finite-pool-solve}
\kekim_gpt{\textbf{R09 --- Keep three distributions distinct throughout the implementation.} The population proof optimizes over the full contextual simplex; a finite-pool solver optimizes categorical weights over sampled responses; the trained LLM defines a distribution over all possible responses. These are not interchangeable objects. Introduce separate notation for the exact inner solution, the finite-pool teacher, and the fitted neural policy. State which one generates each opponent and supplies each surplus in the dual update. This distinction is especially important with only four candidates and $R=1$: a highly accurate teacher solve can coexist with a poor neural projection. It should be possible to trace one outer update from sampled data to teacher weights to neural parameters without inferring undocumented computations.}
\reviewresponse{\textbf{R09 --- Addressed.} The method now separates the population inner solution $\pi_t^\star(\lambda)$, finite-pool target $p_{t,x}^\star(\lambda)$, and neural policy $\pi_\theta$. Finite-pool opponents and prompt-averaged surpluses drive the numerical dual solve; one neural fit follows the frozen target, and its importance-reweighted pool distribution is diagnosed separately.}

At outer stage $t$, draw learner occurrences $\mathcal Y_x=\{y_{x,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_x}$ from $\pi_t$ and comparator occurrences $\mathcal Z_x=\{z_{x,j}\}_{j=1}^{m_x}$ from $\piref$. Write $\widehat A_{k,x}[i,j]=\widehat\Delta_k(y_{x,i},z_{x,j}\mid x)$. Retaining duplicate draws with multiplicity gives empirical base measures $p_{t,x}(i)=1/n_x$ and $\mu_x(j)=1/m_x$. These are importance-sampling occurrence weights, not normalized LM likelihoods. For $p\in\Delta^{n_x}$, define
\begin{align}
\widehat m_{k,x}(p)_j
&:=\sum_{i=1}^{n_x}p_i\widehat A_{k,x}[i,j],\\
\widehat V_{k,x}(p)
&:=-\beta_k\log\sum_{j=1}^{m_x}\mu_{x,j}
\exp\!\left\{-\widehat m_{k,x}(p)_j/\beta_k\right\}.
\label{eq:finite-pool-value}
\end{align}
The fixed disagreement estimate $\widehat d_k$ comes from a reference--reference tensor, not an assumed zero. Let $\widehat{\mathcal D}$ be the empirical prompt distribution and set $\widehat s_k(p)=\E_{x\sim\widehat{\mathcal D}}\widehat V_{k,x}(p_x)-\widehat d_k$. The shared multipliers aggregate these prompt averages; there is no separate Nash bargain at each prompt.

For fixed $\lambda$, the empirical counterpart of \eqref{eq:weighted-policy} separates across prompts:
\begin{equation}
p_{t,x}^\star(\lambda)
\in
\arg\max_{p\in\Delta^{n_x}}
\left\{
\sum_{k=1}^K\lambda_k\widehat V_{k,x}(p)
-\frac{1}{\eta_t}\KL(p\|p_{t,x})
\right\}.
\label{eq:direct-inner}
\end{equation}
Each problem is strictly concave because of the KL term. The opponent is analytically eliminated inside $\widehat V$, so direct optimization accounts for its dependence on $p$. We solve these small programs to numerical tolerance, then solve the shared dual condition $\widehat s_k(p_t^\star(\lambda))=1/\lambda_k$ in $\log\lambda$. A computational box $\Lambda=[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]^K$ requires reporting both unprojected stationarity and bound activity; projected stationarity alone does not certify the original problem. Appendix~\ref{app:prox-dual} gives the residual checks and an explicit instability example for undamped policy--opponent alternation.

Once the dual converges, the finite-pool target defines the pairwise log-ratio change
\begin{equation}
\widehat h_t^\star(x,i,i')
:=
\log\frac{p_{t,x}^\star(i)}{p_{t,x}(i)}
-
\log\frac{p_{t,x}^\star(i')}{p_{t,x}(i')}.
\label{eq:finite-target-logratio}
\end{equation}
For a neural policy $\pi_\theta$, let
\begin{equation}
h_t(\pi_\theta;x,y,y')
:=
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_t(y\mid x)}
-
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y'\mid x)}{\pi_t(y'\mid x)}.
\label{eq:log-ratio-change}
\end{equation}

\reviewresponse{\textbf{R10 --- Partially addressed.} Identification is restricted to connected sampled support, with an explicit unseen-mass counterexample and a separate neural pool diagnostic. We project the finite-pool target into the LLM with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}^{(t)}(\theta)
=
\E_{\substack{x\sim\widehat{\mathcal D}\\(i,i')\sim\xi_x}}
\!\left[
\left(

-\widehat h_t^\star(x,i,i')
\right)^2
\right].
\label{eq:regression-loss}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Nash Bargaining Preference Optimization}
\label{alg:nbpo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Reference $\piref$, pairwise models $P_{1:K}$, temperatures $\beta_{1:K}$, outer stages $T$.
\STATE Set $\pi_0\leftarrow\piref$; construct fixed reference pools and $\widehat d_{1:K}$.
\FOR{$t=0,\ldots,T-1$}
    \STATE Draw learner pools from $\pi_t$, construct cross tensors, initialize $\lambda>0$.
    \REPEAT
        \STATE Solve \eqref{eq:direct-inner} independently for every prompt.
        \STATE Update shared $\lambda$ using $\widehat s_k-1/\lambda_k$; check residuals and bounds.
    \UNTIL{unprojected KKT tolerance is met or the numerical budget is exhausted}
    \STATE On uncertified output, audit feasibility, retain $\pi_t$, and return its failure status.
    \STATE Construct $\widehat h_t^\star$ by \eqref{eq:finite-target-logratio}; verify \eqref{eq:finite-target-identity}.
    \STATE Fit one neural candidate $\widetilde\pi_{t+1}$ using \eqref{eq:regression-loss}.
    \STATE Measure post-fit diagnostics on development data; accept only if declared checks pass.
    \STATE Set $\pi_{t+1}\leftarrow\widetilde\pi_{t+1}$ if accepted, otherwise retain $\pi_t$ and record rejection.
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} $\pi_T$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
Acceptance is empirical, uses development rather than final-test data, and provides no population certificate. Appendix~\ref{app:feasibility} defines failure reporting. Historical runs require artifact verification of this execution contract; all reported LLM runs use $T=1$.

\paragraph{Last-iterate convergence.}

\subsection{Target quality and neural realization}
\label{sec:target-transfer}
The small separation between aggregation targets does not identify whether the remaining bottleneck is target quality or neural realization. We therefore specify a development-only diagnostic on $200$ fixed prompts (Table~\ref{tab:target-transfer}). An independent judge scores the same eight learner candidates against four reference candidates. Without refitting any target, we evaluate the empirical source distribution, each exact target, and the importance-reweighted distribution of a fitted LLM. Fresh generations are evaluated separately against the same reference bank. This isolates whether target improvements survive projection and generation; it does not estimate population exploitability. Protocol and missing-data rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:target-transfer}.

The measured panel separates the three stages. Coverage first: of the $200$ planned prompts, a
prompt-objective cell enters the complete case only when all $76$ of its rubric-order verdicts
parse, and $60{,}252$ of $60{,}800$ verdicts do ($99.10\%$), which leaves $143$, $131$, $144$ and
$167$ complete prompts by objective and $78$ common to all four. Table~\ref{tab:target-transfer}
reports every categorical row on that one common set so the rows stay paired. Because
complete-case selection is not outcome-free, the bounds required by
Appendix~\ref{app:target-transfer} are also computed on all $200$ planned prompts, with each
unresolved preference put at $0$ and at $1$.

The exact targets do carry independent signal. Against the empirical source distribution on the
same eight candidates, the \NBPO{} target gains $+0.0271$ $[+0.0023,+0.0518]$ on instruction
following and $+0.0505$ $[+0.0273,+0.0743]$ on helpfulness, both excluding zero in the
whole-prompt paired bootstrap, while truthfulness $(+0.0030)$ and honesty $(-0.0044)$ contain it;
the minimum finite-bank surplus moves from $-0.0054$ to $+0.0054$. The missing-data bounds make
this robust rather than conditional: over all $200$ prompts the target's win-rate interval lies
entirely above the source's on every objective, for instance $[0.5722,0.5810]$ against
$[0.4898,0.5001]$ on helpfulness. The complete case and the bounds disagree on the sign of the
honesty gap, which is a coverage effect between $78$ and $200$ prompts rather than a contradiction.

What the targets do not do is distinguish the aggregation rules. On the same panel the \NBPO{}
target beats the utilitarian target by $+0.0010$, $+0.0012$, $+0.0018$ and $+0.0004$ across the
four objectives and the fixed-reference target by $-0.0007$ to $+0.0003$; the largest of these is
$0.0018$, against a target-versus-source gap of up to $0.0505$. The null result this paper reports
at the policy level is therefore already present in the targets themselves, before any neural
training and under an evaluator that never saw them.

Projection does not lose that advantage on the sampled support, and it does not reproduce the
target either. The fitted pools score above their own exact targets on instruction following,
truthfulness and honesty and carry a larger minimum surplus ($+0.0222$ for \NBPO{}, $+0.0163$ for
utilitarian, against $+0.0054$ and $+0.0036$), yet they sit further from the target in
distribution than the source they started from: $\KL(p^\star\Vert\widetilde p_\theta)$ averages
$1.457$ for both arms where $\KL(p^\star\Vert p_t)$ is $1.047$ and $1.075$, with total variation
$0.58$ and a log-ratio RMS above $3.2$. The policy moved the pool substantially, in a direction
the independent judge rewards and the regression target does not.

Fresh generations then behave differently by arm. \NBPO{}'s new draws track its fitted pool
($.5455$, $.5460$, $.5221$, $.5781$), the utilitarian arm's fall below its own pool on
instruction following and honesty ($.5158$ against $.5500$, $.4926$ against $.5123$), and the
uniform scalarized DPO arm leads every objective ($.5806$, $.5548$, $.5171$, $.6357$), which is
the ordering Table~\ref{tab:uf-objectives} reports on the final split. No stage is guaranteed to
improve on the previous one, and these are single-seed development rows.

Two judge-contract checks accompany the panel. Thirty-two pairs in the bank carry byte-identical
responses on both sides, and their order-averaged value is exactly $0.5$. Presentation order
changes the verdict on $13{,}312$ of $29{,}893$ pairs, which is why both orders are averaged
rather than sampled. The surplus columns remain a finite-matrix quantity at $m_{\rm eval}=4$ with
$\beta^E=0.25$ fixed before the panel ran, not a population game value; the $[0,1]$ bounds for the
surplus span $[-0.16,+0.18]$ and are uninformative, unlike the direct-win bounds.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{General pairwise preferences.}
NLHF and its scalable variants optimize directly against general pairwise preference models rather than fitting scalar rewards \citep{munos2024nash,wu2025sppo,zhang2025iterative,calandriello2024online,rosset2024direct,zhou2025egpo}. Blackwell-style methods extend this view to vector-valued feedback and target sets \citep{blackwell1956analog,bhatia2020multicriteria}. PROSPER introduces a maximum-entropy Blackwell winner with a scalable single-policy update and an inner worst-case choice over objective weights and opponents \citep{zhang2026prosper}. \NBPO{} instead keeps one regularized game value per objective and applies Nash bargaining across the improvements over a shared fallback.

MODPO, Rewarded Soups, and Panacea expose trade-offs through scalarization, policy interpolation, or preference conditioning \citep{zhou2024beyond,rame2023rewarded,zhong2024panacea}. MaxMin-RLHF and RMOD optimize weakest-objective criteria \citep{chakraborty2024maxmin,son2026rmod}. \subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:game-structure}}
\label{app:proof-game-structure}
For a fixed comparator $\nu$, define
\[
\mathcal J_{k,\nu}(\pi)
:=
J_k(\pi,\nu)
+
\beta_k\E_{x\sim\D}
\KL\!\left(\nu(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\piref(\cdot\mid x)\right).
\]
The first term is linear in $\pi$ and the second does not depend on $\pi$, so $\mathcal J_{k,\nu}$ is affine. The pointwise minimum of affine functions is concave; hence $V_{k,\beta_k}$ is concave. Since $|\Delta_k|\le1/2$,
\[
|V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)-V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi')|
\le
\frac12\E_{x\sim\D}
\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)-\pi'(\cdot\mid x)\|_1,
\]
which also proves continuity.

For $\beta_k>0$, the inner minimization separates across prompts. Fix $x$ and abbreviate $m(z)=m_k^\pi(z\mid x)$. The prompt-wise problem is
\[
\min_{\nu\in\Delta(\Y_x)}
\sum_z\nu(z)m(z)
+
\beta_k\sum_z\nu(z)
\log\frac{\nu(z)}{\piref(z\mid x)}.
\]
The first-order condition under $\sum_z\nu(z)=1$ gives
\[
\nu(z)\propto\piref(z\mid x)\exp\{-m(z)/\beta_k\},
\]
which yields \eqref{eq:regularized-opponent}; substituting it back yields \eqref{eq:soft-value}. The KL term makes the minimizer unique. Danskin's theorem therefore gives, for every feasible direction $h$ satisfying $\sum_yh(y\mid x)=0$,
\[
D V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)[h]
=
\E_{x\sim\D}\sum_y h(y\mid x)
\E_{z\sim\nu^\star_{k,\pi}(\cdot\mid x)}
\Delta_k(y,z\mid x).
\]
Thus the coordinate derivative is
\[
\left[\nabla_\pi V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)\right]_{x,y}
=
\D(x)Q_k^\pi(y\mid x),
\]
proving \eqref{eq:game-gradient}.

\subsection{Connection to the Fixed-Reference}
\label{app:anchor-limit}
This subsection gives a finite-action illustration at the unregularized endpoint $\beta=0$. It is not used in Assumption~\ref{ass:joint}, the full-support analysis, or the finite-temperature experiments.

Define the fixed-reference improvement
\begin{equation}
a_k(\pi)=J_k(\pi,\piref)=P_k(\pi\succ\piref)-\tfrac12.
\label{eq:anchor-margin}
\end{equation}
Skew-symmetry gives $a_k(\piref)=0$.

\paragraph{Full cyclic example.}
For one prompt, let $\piref=\delta_r$, where $\delta_y$ is the deterministic
policy that always outputs $y$. Suppose $A$, $B$, and $C$ each beat $r$
with probability $0.75$ and form the deterministic cycle
\[
A\succ B,\qquad B\succ C,\qquad C\succ A.
\]
Both
\[
\pi_{\rm pure}=\delta_A
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\pi_{\rm mix}=\frac{\delta_A+\delta_B+\delta_C}{3}
\]
have the same fixed-reference improvement,
\[
a(\pi_{\rm pure})
=
a(\pi_{\rm mix})
=
0.75-\frac12
=
0.25.
\]

Their worst-opponent values are nevertheless different. For the pure
policy, response $C$ defeats $A$ deterministically, so
\[
V_0(\pi_{\rm pure})
=
\min_{\nu} J(\pi_{\rm pure},\nu)
=
J(\delta_A,\delta_C)
=
-\frac12.
\]

For the uniform mixture, consider first the pure opponent $\delta_A$.
The mixture outputs $A$, $B$, and $C$ with equal probability. Against
$A$, these responses respectively tie, lose, and win, giving centered
payoffs $0$, $-1/2$, and $+1/2$. Hence
\[
J(\pi_{\rm mix},\delta_A)
=
\frac13
\left(
0-\frac12+\frac12
\right)
=
0.
\]
By symmetry,
\[
J(\pi_{\rm mix},\delta_B)
=
J(\pi_{\rm mix},\delta_C)
=
0.
\]
Against the reference,
\[
J(\pi_{\rm mix},\delta_r)=0.25,
\]
because every response in the mixture beats $r$ with probability $0.75$.
Since $J(\pi_{\rm mix},\nu)$ is linear in $\nu$, no mixed opponent can
achieve a value below the minimum over these pure opponents. Therefore
\[
V_0(\pi_{\rm mix})
=
\min_{\nu}J(\pi_{\rm mix},\nu)
=
\min\{0,0,0,0.25\}
=
0.
\]

Finally, the reference itself has
\[
V_0(\piref)
=
\min_{\nu}J(\delta_r,\nu)
=
-0.25,
\]
since $r$ loses to each of $A$, $B$, and $C$ with probability $0.75$.
Thus
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
& a(\pi) & V_0(\pi) & V_0(\pi)-V_0(\piref)\\
\hline
\pi_{\rm pure} & 0.25 & -0.50 & -0.25\\
\pi_{\rm mix}  & 0.25 & 0     &  0.25
\end{array}
\]
The fixed reference therefore assigns the two policies the same score,
even though the pure policy is fully exploitable by one response whereas
the balanced mixture guarantees an average tie against every response
in the cycle.

\subsection{Policy-Induced Nash Bargaining and Classical Consequences}
\label{app:nash-properties}
We prove Proposition~\ref{prop:nash-outcome}. Since $\Pi$ is compact and each $V_{k,\beta_k}$ is continuous, $V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi)$ is bounded. The definition of $\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}$ then implies boundedness. Closedness follows by taking any convergent sequence $v_n\in\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}$, choosing policies $\pi_n$ with $d\le v_n\le V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi_n)$, extracting a convergent policy subsequence, and applying continuity. Downward closure within $\{v:v\ge d\}$ is immediate from the definition.

For convexity, take $v^1,v^2\in\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}$ with witnesses $\pi_1,\pi_2$. Objective-wise concavity from Proposition~\ref{prop:game-structure} gives, for $\alpha\in[0,1]$,
\[
V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\alpha\pi_1+(1-\alpha)\pi_2)
\ge
\alpha V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi_1)+(1-\alpha)V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi_2)
\]
componentwise, so $\alpha v^1+(1-\alpha)v^2\in\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}$. Assumption~\ref{ass:joint} provides a point strictly above $d$, proving nondegeneracy.

For any $v\in\G_{\boldsymbol\beta}$ with $v>d$, some policy $\pi$ satisfies $v\le V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi)$. Since the Nash objective is coordinatewise increasing,
\[
\sum_k\log(v_k-d_k)
\le
\sum_k\log\bigl(V_{k,\beta_k}(\pi)-d_k\bigr)
=F(\pi).
\]
Conversely, every $\pi\in\Pi_+$ induces a feasible value vector $V_{\boldsymbol\beta}(\pi)$. Hence \eqref{eq:nash-welfare} realizes the classical Nash solution over $(\G_{\boldsymbol\beta},d)$.

The remaining statements are standard Nash-bargaining consequences, but we record the short arguments for completeness. Since the log objective tends to $-\infty$ when any surplus approaches zero, every optimizer lies strictly above $d$. Coordinatewise monotonicity implies Pareto optimality. Strict concavity of $\sum_k\log s_k$ over value vectors implies that all optimal policies induce the same surplus, hence the same game-value vector, although the policy itself can be nonunique.

Finally, under independent positive affine transformations
\[
V_k'(\pi)=c_kV_k(\pi)+b_k,
\qquad
d_k'=c_kd_k+b_k,
\qquad c_k>0,
\]
the transformed surplus is $s_k'(\pi)=c_ks_k(\pi)$ and
\[
\sum_k\log s_k'(\pi)
=
\sum_k\log s_k(\pi)+\sum_k\log c_k.
\]
The added term is independent of $\pi$, so the optimizer set is unchanged.

\paragraph{Scope of invariance and alternatives.}
Affine invariance transforms completed utilities and disagreement values together. At the primitive level, $A'_k=c_k A_k$ requires $\beta'_k=c_k\beta_k$ to preserve the opponent and obtain $V'_k=c_kV_k$. With fixed temperature instead, $V_{cA,\beta}=cV_{A,\beta/c}$. If scaled margins leave $[-1/2,1/2]$, the transformation changes payoff units rather than defining a new probability oracle. Arbitrary judge recalibration is not covered.

Exact dual multipliers transform as $\lambda'_k=\lambda_k/c_k$, leaving $\lambda'_kQ'_k=\lambda_kQ_k$ and the pair target unchanged. Matching projected-gradient trajectories further requires scaling initial multipliers and coordinate bounds by $1/c_k$, any diagnostic surplus thresholds by $c_k$, and coordinate step sizes by $1/c_k^2$. Target signs alone do not verify these stronger identities. We do not floor surpluses to make infeasible targets positive.

A Pareto-refining tie-break can remove the dominated-optimum failure of raw maxmin. Relative-improvement bargaining provides another scale-invariant choice but requires objective-wise ideal values and, in general, an appropriate refinement \citep{kalai1975,han2026fair}. These alternatives do not make Nash uniquely admissible. Here the bargaining parties are objective rubrics: duplicating a rubric duplicates its log-surplus term. The fixed reference also plays two distinct roles, disagreement policy and opponent anchor; changing both changes the utilities as well as disagreement.

The positive guarantees in Table~\ref{tab:rule-guarantees} follow
directly from the corresponding objectives. Because $w_k>0$, the
positive-weight utilitarian objective $\sum_k w_k V_k(\pi)$ is strictly
increasing in every game-value coordinate, so none of its optimizers can
be Pareto dominated. Under Assumption~\ref{ass:joint}, surplus maxmin
also attains a strictly positive minimum surplus because a jointly
improving policy exists. The examples below establish the guarantees
that can fail for the alternative rules.

All examples use three responses, so a policy is a probability vector
$\pi=(\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3)\in\Delta_3$, and use the uniform reference
$\piref=(1/3,1/3,1/3)$. For objective $k$, $\Delta_k$ is the skew-symmetric
pairwise payoff matrix: $(\Delta_k)_{ij}$ is the centered preference margin of response
$i$ against response $j$. At $\beta_k=0$,
\[
V_k(\pi)
=
\min_j(\pi^\top \Delta_k)_j,
\qquad
d_k=V_k(\piref),
\qquad
s_k(\pi)=V_k(\pi)-d_k.
\]
Thus $\pi^\top \Delta_k$ contains the expected payoff of $\pi$ against each
pure opponent, and $V_k(\pi)$ is its smallest coordinate.

Because each $V_k$ is the minimum of linear functions, the optimizers
below are obtained from small linear programs. For example,
equal-weight utilitarian optimization can be written as
\[
\max_{\pi,t_1,t_2}\ t_1+t_2
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
t_k\le(\pi^\top \Delta_k)_j
\ \ \forall k,j,\qquad
\pi\in\Delta_3.
\]
Absolute maxmin uses one variable $t$ with
$t\le(\pi^\top \Delta_k)_j$, while surplus maxmin uses
$t+d_k\le(\pi^\top \Delta_k)_j$ for every $k,j$.

Joint improvement is feasible, as witnessed by
\[
\bar\pi=(1/3,7/15,1/5),
\]
for which
\[
V(\bar\pi)=(-4/15,-1/60)
\]
and therefore
\[
s(\bar\pi)=(1/15,1/15)>0.
\]

Absolute maxmin instead solves
\[
\max_{\pi}\min\{V_1(\pi),V_2(\pi)\},
\]
whose linear-program solution is
\[
\pi_{\rm AM}=(0,4/5,1/5).
\]
For this policy,
\[
\pi_{\rm AM}^\top \Delta_1=(7/20,-1/10,2/5),
\qquad
\pi_{\rm AM}^\top \Delta_2=(-1/10,-1/10,2/5),
\]
so
\[
V(\pi_{\rm AM})=(-1/10,-1/10)
\]
and
\[
s(\pi_{\rm AM})
=
(7/30,-1/60).
\]
Thus absolute maxmin maximizes the smallest \emph{absolute} game value
while allowing objective~2 to fall below its own fallback.

\paragraph{A Pareto-dominated surplus-maxmin optimum.}
Let
\[
\Delta_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1/2&1/2\\
1/2&0&1/2\\
-1/2&-1/2&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Delta_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&1/2\\
0&0&1/2\\
-1/2&-1/2&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The uniform reference gives
\[
d=(-1/3,-1/6).
\]
For objective~2, no policy can achieve $V_2(\pi)>0$, while every mixture
supported on the first two responses achieves $V_2(\pi)=0$.
Consequently,
\[
s_2(\pi)\le 1/6
\]
for every policy, so the optimal surplus-maxmin value cannot exceed
$1/6$.

The two policies
\[
\pi_c=(1/3,2/3,0),
\qquad
\pi_e=(0,1,0)
\]
satisfy
\[
V(\pi_c)=(-1/6,0),
\qquad
s(\pi_c)=(1/6,1/6),
\]
and
\[
V(\pi_e)=(0,0),
\qquad
s(\pi_e)=(1/3,1/6).
\]
Both therefore maximize
\[
\min_k s_k(\pi)
\]
at $1/6$. However,
\[
s(\pi_e)>s(\pi_c)
\]
in the first coordinate and is equal in the second, so
$\pi_e$ Pareto dominates $\pi_c$. Thus the surplus-maxmin optimizer set
can contain Pareto-dominated policies.

\subsection{Dual Derivation, Direct Solve, and Neural Projection}
\label{app:prox-dual}
For multipliers $\lambda_k\ge0$, the Lagrangian of \eqref{eq:prox-subproblem} is
\[
\mathcal L_t(\pi,u,\lambda)
=
\sum_k\log u_k
+
\sum_k\lambda_k\bigl(s_k(\pi)-u_k\bigr)
-
\eta_t^{-1}D(\pi\|\pi_t).
\]
For fixed $\lambda_k>0$, maximizing $\log u_k-\lambda_ku_k$ gives $u_k=1/\lambda_k$ and value $-\log\lambda_k-1$; if $\lambda_k=0$, the maximum is unbounded. Substitution yields \eqref{eq:prox-dual}, and the remaining maximization over $\pi$ is \eqref{eq:weighted-policy}. Uniqueness of that maximizer and Danskin's theorem yield \eqref{eq:dual-gradient}.

Assumption~\ref{ass:joint} provides strict feasibility: choose $\bar\pi$ with $s_k(\bar\pi)>0$ and $0<\bar u_k<s_k(\bar\pi)$. Slater's condition therefore gives strong duality. Stationarity in $u_k$ gives
\[
\lambda_{t+1,k}=\frac{1}{u_{t+1,k}}>0,
\]
and complementary slackness then forces $u_{t+1,k}=s_k(\pi_{t+1})$. This proves Proposition~\ref{prop:prox-dual}.

\paragraph{Dual step used as a fallback.}
Stationarity $\widehat s_k(p_t^\star(\lambda))=1/\lambda_k$ can also be approached by projected subgradient \emph{descent} on the dual --- the update below subtracts the dual gradient, and the dual of the concave inner problem is minimized over $\lambda$ ---
\begin{equation}
\lambda^{(m+1)}
=
\Pi_{\Lambda}\!\left[
\lambda^{(m)}-
\gamma_m\left(
\widehat s\!\left(p_t^\star(\lambda^{(m)})\right)
-(\lambda^{(m)})^{-1}
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:dual-update}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_m>0$ is the dual step size, $\Pi_{\Lambda}$ is Euclidean projection onto $\Lambda=[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]^K$ with $0<\lambda_{\min}<\lambda_{\max}$, and the inverse is elementwise. On the pools reported here this iteration stalls at a projected KKT residual near $10^{-2}$ after $3000$ steps, whereas the root solve reaches $10^{-9}$ in tens of inner solves; no reported result uses \eqref{eq:dual-update}.

\paragraph{What the projected certificate certifies.}
The projected residual concerns the box-constrained dual. Original stationarity additionally requires $\|\widehat s-\lambda^{-1}\|_\infty$ to meet tolerance; active bounds cannot be ignored merely because projection removes the residual. We record each bound hit and classify a solve with unresolved unprojected stationarity as uncertified. Interior numerical solutions with small residuals support the finite problem only. The supplied SafeRLHF report gives $\Lambda=[10^{-3},10^3]^2$, $\lambda=(6.33,6.19)$, and independently recomputed unprojected residual $6.7\times10^{-11}$. These are teacher diagnostics, not neural-policy certificates.

\paragraph{Finite-pool optimality and neural projection.}
Fix $\lambda$ and let $\pi_t^\star(\lambda)$ solve \eqref{eq:weighted-policy}. First-order optimality gives, for every prompt and response pair in the support,
\begin{equation}
h_t(\pi_t^\star(\lambda);x,y,y')
=
\eta_t\sum_k\lambda_k
\left(
Q_k^{\pi_t^\star(\lambda)}(y\mid x)
-
Q_k^{\pi_t^\star(\lambda)}(y'\mid x)
\right).
\label{eq:prox-pairwise-kkt}
\end{equation}
The direct finite-pool program \eqref{eq:direct-inner} is the sampled analogue of this strictly concave problem. Its normalized optimizer determines all finite-pool log-probability differences, so \eqref{eq:finite-target-logratio} is identified without computing a language-model normalizer. Equation~\eqref{eq:finite-target-identity} is the corresponding finite-pool KKT identity and is checked numerically before an artifact is accepted. If the positive-probability pair graph connects all sampled occurrences, zero regression error implies
\[
\widetilde p_{\theta,x}(i)
=\frac{p_{t,x}(i)\,\pi_\theta(y_{x,i}\mid x)/\pi_t(y_{x,i}\mid x)}
{\sum_j p_{t,x}(j)\,\pi_\theta(y_{x,j}\mid x)/\pi_t(y_{x,j}\mid x)}
=p_{t,x}^\star(i).
\]
This identifies the importance-reweighted occurrence measure only. For example, under a uniform three-response anchor, fitting only pair $(1,2)$ with target zero permits every neural distribution $(a,a,1-2a)$, $0<a<1/2$. Unseen-response mass remains unconstrained. Resampling a new learner pool at each outer stage makes the empirical base measure track $\pi_t$; reusing a pool from another proposal instead requires explicit importance weights. The reference opponent anchor remains $\piref$.

\paragraph{Why an optimal fixed point need not be reached.}
\label{app:direct-stability}
Use the cyclic matrix $A$ above, reference and proximal center $\mu=(.34,.33,.33)$, $\beta=.25$, and fixed $\eta\lambda=4$. Undamped best-response alternation applies
\[
T(p)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\log\mu+
4A\operatorname{softmax}(\log\mu+Ap/\beta)\right).
\]
Its fixed point is $p^\star=(.33618258,.32907171,.33474571)$, but both tangent-space Jacobian eigenvalues are approximately $-1.333163$. Perturbations therefore alternate and expand locally. This is not excluded by improvement: $V(p^\star)-V(\mu)\simeq4.7194\times10^{-5}>0$. In the supplied deterministic check, $100$ iterations from $\mu$ leave $\|p-p^\star\|_2\simeq.27986$; a direct constrained solve of the same fixed-multiplier problem reaches inner stationarity below $10^{-9}$. These are finite-game calculations, not LLM results or a complete Nash dual solve.

For general fixed $\lambda$, let $f_x(p;\lambda)$ denote the objective in \eqref{eq:direct-inner} and let
$\nu_k(p)=\operatorname{softmax}(\log\mu_x-\widehat A_{k,x}^{\top}p/\beta_k)$. On the simplex interior,
\begin{align*}
\nabla f_x(p;\lambda)
&=\sum_k\lambda_k\widehat A_{k,x}\nu_k(p)
-\eta_t^{-1}\bigl(\log(p/p_{t,x})+\mathbf1\bigr),\\
\nabla^2 f_x(p;\lambda)
&=-\sum_k\frac{\lambda_k}{\beta_k}\widehat A_{k,x}
\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(\nu_k)-\nu_k\nu_k^\top\bigr)\widehat A_{k,x}^\top
-\eta_t^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(1/p)\prec0.
\end{align*}
Thus the finite target is unique, and direct optimization includes opponent feedback through this curvature. Strict concavity does not certify an arbitrary numerical routine: simplex feasibility and inner/dual residuals still require measurement. The empirical tensor itself remains an approximation to population preferences; exponentiation, normalization, and log-sum-exp do not preserve unbiasedness of noisy margins.

\paragraph{Finite-pool feasibility and failure reporting.}
\label{app:feasibility}
When diagnosing an uncertified solve, use the finite-pool program
\[
\widehat\tau^\star=\max_{\{p_x\},\tau}\tau
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\E_{x\sim\widehat{\mathcal D}}\widehat V_{k,x}(p_x)-\widehat d_k\ge\tau\ (\forall k),
\qquad p_x\in\Delta^{n_x}.
\]
This is convex optimization with concave-superlevel constraints, not generally a linear program at $\beta_k>0$. A verified positive feasible margin witnesses strict finite-pool improvement. Only a valid nonpositive upper bound certifies its absence; failure to find a witness is unresolved. A tolerance-bounded interval near zero means no resolved positive margin, not exact infeasibility. Independent learner and reference banks need not contain a categorical representation of the fallback, so finite-pool infeasibility is not population infeasibility.

For an analytic infeasible example, take two actions, $\mu=(1/2,1/2)$, $A_1=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1/2\\-1/2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $A_2=-A_1$. For $p=(a,1-a)$ and any common $\beta>0$, $s_1(p)=(a-1/2)/2=-s_2(p)$, hence $\tau^\star=0$. No clipping or inverse-surplus floor creates a strictly feasible Nash target.

The execution contract records feasible, certified-infeasible, numerical-unresolved, and neural-rejected outcomes separately. Uncertified solves retain the previous policy. This audit introduces no separate neural warm-start stage. Post-fit acceptance uses prespecified development checks, including empirical surplus signs, target fit, and numerical validity; reused monitoring prompts are validation data. False acceptance/rejection rates near the boundary remain an unmeasured audit, and generic bounded-mean confidence formulas do not automatically cover biased game-value estimates.

\subsection{Last-Iterate Convergence}
\label{app:convergence}
\begin{theorem}[Last-iterate convergence]
\label{thm:last-iterate}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:joint}, let $\pi^\star$ maximize \eqref{eq:nash-welfare}, set $\pi_0=\piref$, and solve \eqref{eq:prox-subproblem} exactly at every outer iteration with arbitrary $\eta_t>0$. Then $F(\pi_{t+1})\ge F(\pi_t)$ for $t\ge1$, and for every $T\ge1$,
\begin{equation}
F(\pi^\star)-F(\pi_T)
\le
\frac{D(\pi^\star\|\piref)}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_t}.
\label{eq:last-iterate-rate}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Work throughout in the $\D$-weighted geometry: pair functions of $(x,y)$ by
\[
\langle f,g\rangle_\D=\E_{x\sim\D}\sum_yf(x,y)g(x,y),
\]
and let $h$ be the \emph{prompt-wise} negative entropy $h(\pi)(x)=\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)\log\pi(y\mid x)$, whose gradient at $(x,y)$ is $1+\log\pi(y\mid x)$. Its Bregman divergence in that geometry is exactly $D(\pi\|\pi_t)=\E_{x\sim\D}\KL(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_t(\cdot\mid x))$. The prompt expectation enters once, through the pairing, and is not also carried inside $h$. Because the objective is increasing in $u$, the policy part of \eqref{eq:prox-subproblem} is equivalently
\begin{equation}
\pi_{t+1}
\in
\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_+}
\left\{F(\pi)-\frac1{\eta_t}D(\pi\|\pi_t)\right\}.
\label{eq:implicit-prox}
\end{equation}
The feasible set is nonempty by Assumption~\ref{ass:joint}, and $F(\pi)\to-\infty$ as any surplus approaches zero. Thus the maximizer exists in $\Pi_+$ even when the center $\pi_t$ is the reference $\piref\notin\Pi_+$. Because $\pi_t$ has full support and the remaining objective has finite directional derivatives at an interior surplus point, the inward derivative of $-D(\pi\|\pi_t)$ excludes zero-probability coordinates. Induction therefore gives a full-support, positive-surplus $\pi_{t+1}$.

Let $g_{t+1}$ be a supergradient of $F$ at $\pi_{t+1}$. First-order optimality of \eqref{eq:implicit-prox} and concavity of $F$ imply, for every $\pi\in\Pi_+$,
\begin{align}
F(\pi)-F(\pi_{t+1})
&\le \langle g_{t+1},\pi-\pi_{t+1}\rangle_\D\\
&\le\frac1{\eta_t}
\left\langle\nabla h(\pi_{t+1})-\nabla h(\pi_t),
\pi-\pi_{t+1}\right\rangle_\D\\
&=\frac1{\eta_t}
\left[
D(\pi\|\pi_t)-D(\pi\|\pi_{t+1})-D(\pi_{t+1}\|\pi_t)
\right].
\label{eq:prox-three-point}
\end{align}
For $t\ge1$, $\pi_t$ is feasible in the next subproblem. Comparing the optimum with $\pi_t$ gives
\begin{equation}
F(\pi_{t+1})-\frac{1}{\eta_t}D(\pi_{t+1}\|\pi_t)
\ge F(\pi_t),
\label{eq:prox-monotone}
\end{equation}
so the objective values are nondecreasing from $\pi_1$ onward.

Set $\pi=\pi^\star$ in \eqref{eq:prox-three-point}, multiply by $\eta_t$, and sum from $t=0$ to $T-1$:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_t
\big(F(\pi^\star)-F(\pi_{t+1})\big)
\le
D(\pi^\star\|\piref).
\label{eq:prox-telescope}
\end{equation}
Because the gaps are nonincreasing, the left-hand side is at least
$\big(\sum_t\eta_t\big)(F(\pi^\star)-F(\pi_T))$, which proves \eqref{eq:last-iterate-rate}.

\begin{corollary}[Convergence of the surplus profile]
\label{cor:surplus-convergence}
Under the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:last-iterate}, let
$s^\star=s(\pi^\star)$ denote the unique surplus vector induced by a
Nash-optimal policy. Then
\begin{equation}
\|s(\pi_T)-s^\star\|_2^2
\le
\frac{2D(\pi^\star\|\piref)}
{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_t}.
\label{eq:last-iterate-surplus}
\end{equation}
Hence a constant proximal step gives
$\|s(\pi_T)-s^\star\|_2=O(T^{-1/2})$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This is a stronger consequence of Nash-welfare convergence rather than a
separate requirement for the NBPO update. For every objective,
$V_k(\pi)\in[-1/2,1/2]$, and
$d_k=V_k(\piref)\le0$ because $\nu=\piref$ is feasible and
$J_k(\piref,\piref)=0$. Hence every positive surplus is at most one. On
$(0,1]^K$, the function
$G(s)=\sum_k\log s_k$ is $1$-strongly concave in Euclidean norm.
Proposition~\ref{prop:nash-outcome} implies that all Nash-optimal policies induce the
same game-value vector, and therefore the same surplus vector $s^\star$.
First-order optimality over the convex comprehensive surplus set induced by
\eqref{eq:comprehensive-hull} gives
\begin{equation}
G(s^\star)-G(s)
\ge
\frac12\|s-s^\star\|_2^2.
\label{eq:surplus-strong-concavity}
\end{equation}
Applying this inequality to $s=s(\pi_T)$ and using
\eqref{eq:last-iterate-rate} proves \eqref{eq:last-iterate-surplus}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Preference graphs and metrics.}
For each correctly oriented judgment, assign $X=1$ to a win, $X=0$ to a loss, and $X=1/2$ to a tie. For panel $b\in\{A,B\}$, define
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{(b)}_{ij}
=\tfrac12\left(\overline X^{(b,\,i\text{-first})}_{ij}
+\overline X^{(b,\,j\text{-first})}_{ij}\right).
\label{eq:audit-preference}
\end{equation}
This is a tie-adjusted preference under equal presentation-order sampling, conditional on the fixed judge, rather than a human-population probability. An edge estimate requires all ten scheduled judgments; otherwise it is unresolved. Draw $i\to j$ only when $\widehat p^{(b)}_{ij}>1/2$; exact ties create no edge. Let $C_A$ count prompts containing any directed cycle in panel A, including length-four cycles, and let $R$ count those containing the \emph{same oriented response triangle} in both panels. The latter measures repeatability, not statistical confirmation. Let $W_A$ count prompts where every response has an observed loss; this rules out a strict winner in the observed graph. Otherwise, a graph without an observed strict winner is unresolved. Report all three rates over the $100$ planned prompts, and cyclic-triangle rates over the $400$ planned triangles. Missing comparisons cannot establish an event; report unresolved counts and the corresponding worst-case upper rate, without interpreting unobserved cycles as absent. Let $n$ be the number of tournaments complete in both panels.

\paragraph{BT sampling-noise control.}
A noisy transitive model can also produce observed cycles. Fit an order-aware BT model with a tie extension to panel A, with one score $r_i$ per response, a rubric-level position coefficient $b$, and tie parameter $\nu>0$:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(i,\mathrm{tie},j\mid o)
\ \propto\ \left(e^{z/2},\,2\nu,\,e^{-z/2}\right),
\qquad z=r_i-r_j+bo,\quad o\in\{-1,+1\}.
\label{eq:audit-bt-null}
\end{equation}
Here $o=+1$ presents $i$ first; response scores sum to zero within each prompt. Fit by summed negative log likelihood with a fixed $10^{-3}$ squared penalty on scores, $b$, and $\log\nu$ to keep separated fits finite. Under equal-order averaging this null retains a transitive latent ranking. Generate $2000$ synthetic A/B panel pairs under the fitted null, preserving presentation orders, repeat counts, and missingness, and rerun the graph construction. Report its mean repeated-cycle rate $R_{\mathrm{BT}}$, the excess $\Delta R=R-R_{\mathrm{BT}}$, and the upper-tail value $(1+\#\{R^{\mathrm{sim}}\ge R\})/2001$. These are approximate fitted-null checks, not exact composite-null tests. Apply Holm correction across the four individual rubrics; report the joint condition separately. Obtain $95\%$ intervals with $2000$ whole-prompt bootstrap replicates, retaining all judgments and refitting the null. These intervals measure prompt variability, not all uncertainty in the fitted noise model. Pairs and triangles are not independent samples.

\paragraph{Independent confirmation of a cycle.}
Before opening B, select at most one directed cyclic triangle per individual rubric from A. Require complete A judgments and all three cycle directions to agree across both presentation orders. Among eligible cycles, maximize the minimum edge margin, breaking ties by prompt and response IDs. Fix its orientation and allocate $200$ independent B judgments per selected edge, $100$ per order. This adds at most $4\times3\times190=2280$ judgments; only the first five B judgments per presentation order enter the aggregate tables and their null simulations. For each selected edge $e$, use the full $m=200$ bounded observations to test its order-averaged population mean against $1/2$:
\begin{equation}
p_e=\exp\!\left[-2m\left(\max\{\widehat p_e-1/2,0\}\right)^2\right],
\qquad p_{\mathrm{cycle}}=\max_{e\text{ in triangle}}p_e.
\label{eq:audit-cycle-test}
\end{equation}
Hoeffding's bound gives a conservative edge test under independent judgments \citep{hoeffding1963probability}. The cycle null states that at least one of its three edges has mean at most $1/2$, so the maximum edge $p$-value tests that union null. Apply Holm at $0.05$ across the four rubric witnesses, assigning $p=1$ when no witness is selected or confirmation is incomplete. This establishes a cycle only for the specified judge's order-averaged preference distribution. Require no adaptively chosen extra samples after viewing B. Report both order-specific means, and release the prompt, rubric, response texts, and individual judgments.

\subsection{Preference-model diagnostics}
\paragraph{Anti-symmetric general preference model.}
For objective $k$, a joint encoder produces $a_{\phi,k}(x,y,z)$ and we set
\[
\ell_{\phi,k}(x,y,z)
=\tfrac12\{a_{\phi,k}(x,y,z)-a_{\phi,k}(x,z,y)\},
\qquad
P_{\phi,k}(y\succ z\mid x)=\sigma(\ell_{\phi,k}(x,y,z)).
\]
This guarantees exact swap complementarity and self-ties without constraining the logit to a scalar reward difference. In the SafeRLHF diagnostic, both the joint model and the scalar BT baseline use RoBERTa-base so that encoder capacity and training cost are matched; this backbone choice is an experimental control rather than part of the NBPO method. Because the observed SafeRLHF graph contains no triangles, their near-null comparison is a calibration result rather than evidence that human preferences are transitive.

\paragraph{Aggregation across objectives and across prompts.}
NBPO applies $\sum_k\log(V_k(\pi)-d_k)$ to prompt-averaged objective values. In contrast, PROSPER's regularized criterion has prompt-dependent weights \citep{zhang2026prosper}:
\[
\min_{w:\mathcal X\to\Delta_K,\,\nu}
\E_x\!\left[
\sum_k w_k(x)J_{k,x}(\pi,\nu)
+\beta\KL(\nu(\cdot\mid x)\|\piref(\cdot\mid x))\right],
\]
where $J_{k,x}$ is the conditional pairwise payoff. Constant shifts from centered payoffs do not change its optimizer. Prompt-dependent weights cannot be replaced by a shared weight without changing this criterion. Prompt-direct optimization decomposes only the inner computation; NBPO's $\lambda$ remains shared across prompts.

To illustrate aggregation order alone, take two equally likely prompts. Surplus vectors $(.2,0)$ and $(0,.2)$ average to $(.1,.1)$, whereas $(.08,.08)$ on both prompts averages to $(.08,.08)$. Global Nash prefers the first policy, while the average prompt-wise minimum prefers the second. This schematic example omits opponent regularization and is not a numerical PROSPER result.

The reported PROSPER adaptation uses prompt-wise absolute-maxmin aggregation at $\beta=0.25$ and matches coefficient mass to the Nash dual ($33.92$). The source reports certified solutions on $11{,}000$ train/dev prompts and a maximum log-ratio identity residual of $9.9\times10^{-11}$. Training objective-weight means are $(6.97,12.03,7.56,7.36)$ with SDs $(1.93,4.09,1.63,3.14)$; the mean effective number of active objectives is $3.50$. These measurements distinguish prompt-dependent weights from a shared vector, but do not by themselves establish a faithful reproduction of every published training step.

The selected target has a negative minimum per-prompt surplus on $642/10{,}000$ training prompts ($6.42\%$) and $49/1{,}000$ development prompts ($4.90\%$). These are selected-policy violation rates, not infeasibility certificates. A different policy may jointly improve the objectives; certifying otherwise requires a nonpositive upper bound from the separate maximum-margin problem in Appendix~\ref{app:prox-dual}. Aggregate and per-prompt improvement remain distinct. Generated-policy results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:uf-objectives}.

\paragraph{Alternative realization.}
For comparison with \eqref{eq:regression-loss}, weighted behavior cloning uses
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{WBC}}^{(t)}(\theta)
=\E_x\sum_{i=1}^{n_x}p_{t,x}^\star(i)
\bigl[-\log\pi_\theta(y_{x,i}\mid x)\bigr].
\label{eq:wbc-loss}
\end{equation}
Weights are detached and full-vocabulary response log-probabilities are summed over tokens. With IID reference-policy occurrences, this weighted likelihood approximates population forward-KL projection; it remains a finite-sample realization rather than an exact population update \citep{agnihotri2026mopo}. Averaging $\frac{n_x}{2}[p_i^\star(-\log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x))+p_j^\star(-\log\pi_\theta(y_j\mid x))]$ over all unordered pairs equals the prompt-wise objective. Uniform teacher weights need not produce zero gradient on a finite sampled dataset.

\paragraph{Metrics and selection history.}
Write $t=\widehat h_t^\star$ and $h=h_t(\pi_\theta)$. We report $\mathrm{nMSE}=\E[(h-t)^2]/\E[t^2]$, sign agreement, Pearson and Spearman correlations. Expectations use the stated split and pair measure. The zero predictor has nMSE $1$. The reported oracle rescaling diagnostic is
\[
a^\star=\frac{\E[ht]}{\E[h^2]},\qquad
\mathrm{nMSE}^\star
=\min_{a\in\R}\frac{\E[(ah-t)^2]}{\E[t^2]}.
\]
It fits a scalar on the evaluation observations and can reverse sign. It is neither centered explained variance nor a deployable calibration result. A direction-preserving version restricts $a\ge0$; signed coefficients and this additional metric require the raw moments. If $\E[h^2]=0$, set $a^\star=0$ and the residual to $1$. Mean response log-ratio is measured under the sampled occurrence distribution; it is not total pool probability or a full-policy KL certificate.

\paragraph{Finite-pool distribution fidelity.}

\[
\widehat p_\theta(i\mid x)
=\frac{p_{t,x}(i)e^{\Delta_i}}{\sum_j p_{t,x}(j)e^{\Delta_j}}.
\]
This self-normalized importance distribution is evaluated against the solved target by forward KL on the fixed sampled support. It does not measure the full LLM divergence or probability outside that support. MSE-250 and WBC-250 reduce KL by $0.1675$ and $0.1004$, respectively; the reported intervals in Table~\ref{tab:pool-drift} concern Pearson correlation, not KL improvement. \subsection{Independent cross-play and finite-bank diagnostics}
\label{app:crossplay}
Within each panel, fix the evaluator, rubric wording, parsing, response sampling, and held-out prompt IDs before comparisons. The comparator bank contains the base, NBPO, fixed-reference Nash, and one strong competitor selected on development data. Use fresh responses outside the training pool and both presentation orders. Comparator selection does not access final outcomes. Table~\ref{tab:crossplay-matrices} records each rubric separately; six unordered comparisons per rubric supply the off-diagonal entries, with reversed cells obtained by antisymmetry and the diagonal fixed at $0.5$ by convention rather than measured self-play.

For a generated policy pair, let $\widehat J_k(\pi,q)=\widehat W_k(\pi,q)-1/2$. A complementary policy-bank diagnostic is
\[
 \widehat V_k^{\mathcal B}(\pi)
 =\min_{w\in\Delta(\mathcal B)}
 \left\{\sum_{q\in\mathcal B}w_q\widehat J_k(\pi,q)
        +\beta_k\KL(w\|a)\right\},
 \qquad
 \widehat s_k^{\mathcal B}(\pi)
 =\widehat V_k^{\mathcal B}(\pi)-\widehat V_k^{\mathcal B}(\piref).
\]
Here $a$ is a full-support distribution over the comparator policies, fixed on development data and shared by every evaluated policy; $\beta_k$ is also fixed within the panel. Record both before evaluating this diagnostic. This is a regularized finite-policy-bank game. Its KL is over comparator weights and is not the response-distribution KL in \eqref{eq:game-value}; consequently it is not a direct estimate of the population value $V_k$ or population exploitability. Training-teacher values are reported separately.

Report every policy seed, then mean and standard deviation across training seeds. Each paired prompt-bootstrap replicate recomputes objective means and then the minimum. Its intervals condition on a checkpoint and do not include training-seed variability. Freeze development choices before confirmatory comparisons and retain the history of benchmark exposure. SafeRLHF Nash/utilitarian aggregates and UF neural diagnostics use three policy seeds; UF generated-policy seed counts vary by method as reported in Table~\ref{tab:uf-objectives}; historical WBC/long-schedule snapshots use one. Missing method-wise values must not be reconstructed from ranges pooled across methods; dashes are unmeasured, not zeros. All result updates must record checkpoint, split, decoding, evaluator, sample count, and source artifact. Unmatched literature scores do not fill matched-baseline rows.


\section{Target-to-Policy Diagnostic Protocol}
\label{app:target-transfer}
This development-only diagnostic leaves completed UF-4 results unchanged. Fix $200$ policy-dev prompts and $200$ disjoint training prompts by an outcome-independent rule. Record IDs and model, tokenizer, template and decoding revisions. A reduced budget is a separately declared pilot, not a silent replacement.

\paragraph{Independent scoring on common support.}
Retain the eight learner occurrences $Y_x$, frozen targets and shared training multipliers; do not refit a $200$-prompt bargain. Select four reference occurrences $Z_x^E$ independently of outcomes, preserving duplicate multiplicity. Independent evaluation supplies

\[
 A^E_{k,x}[i,j]=\widehat P^E_k(y_{x,i}\succ z^E_{x,j}\mid x)-\tfrac12,
 \qquad
 B^E_{k,x}[\ell,j]=\widehat P^E_k(z^E_{x,\ell}\succ z^E_{x,j}\mid x)-\tfrac12.
\]
Average both orders for each reference pair, set actual self-pairs to zero and reconstruct reversed pairs by antisymmetry. Cross-matrix index transposition is not response reversal. Evaluation scores never refit targets or select weights.

With $\mu_j^E=1/4$ and one common evaluation temperature $\beta_k^E=0.25$, define
\begin{align}
 W_k^E(p)&=\E_x\left[\tfrac12+\sum_{i,j}p_{x,i}\mu_j^E A^E_{k,x}[i,j]\right],\label{eq:transfer-wins}\\
 V^E_{k,x}(p)&=-\beta_k^E\log\sum_j\mu_j^E
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{\sum_i p_{x,i}A^E_{k,x}[i,j]}{\beta_k^E}\right\},\label{eq:transfer-value}\\
 d^E_{k,x}&=-\beta_k^E\log\sum_j\mu_j^E
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{\sum_\ell\mu_\ell^E B^E_{k,x}[\ell,j]}{\beta_k^E}\right\},\qquad
 s_k^E(p)=\E_x[V^E_{k,x}(p)-d^E_{k,x}].\label{eq:transfer-surplus}
\end{align}
These are finite-matrix evaluations, not unbiased population game-value estimates. Reference wins and surplus are $.5$ and $0$ by convention. The empirical learner distribution $p_{t,x}$ is different and must not receive these values by definition. Training temperatures and targets remain unchanged.

\paragraph{Fitted pool versus generated policy.}
For each frozen full-run checkpoint, evaluate
\[
 \widetilde p_{\theta,x}(i)
 =\frac{p_{t,x}(i)\exp\{\log\pi_\theta(y_{x,i}\mid x)-\log\pi_t(y_{x,i}\mid x)\}}
 {\sum_{i'}p_{t,x}(i')\exp\{\log\pi_\theta(y_{x,i'}\mid x)-\log\pi_t(y_{x,i'}\mid x)\}}.
\]
Sum log probabilities over identical response tokens and terminal-token conventions. Raw-likelihood normalization is not this diagnostic. Fresh rows compare one new stochastic response per prompt with the same four references under common decoding. Report direct wins; one draw does not estimate the nonlinear stochastic-policy game value, so its surplus is n/a.

\paragraph{Coverage and interpretation.}
Report planned/common-complete counts and invalid rates by rubric and order. High per-call parsing accuracy need not imply complete prompts. After uniform repair, retain unresolved entries as missing. Bound their probabilities in $[0,1]$; combine lower policy and upper reference values for a surplus lower bound, and conversely for its upper bound. Complete-case scores are conditional estimates. Use paired difference intervals; containing zero does not establish equivalence.

\paragraph{Equivalent all-candidate regression.}
On a prompt with $n$ learner occurrences define
\[
 u_i=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{x,i}\mid x)}{\pi_t(y_{x,i}\mid x)}
       -\log\frac{p^\star_{t,x}(i)}{p_{t,x}(i)},\qquad
 \bar u=\frac1n\sum_i u_i.
\]
For uniform unordered pairs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:regression-loss} can be evaluated exactly as
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{\binom n2}\sum_{i<i'}(u_i-u_{i'})^2
 =\frac2{n-1}\sum_i(u_i-\bar u)^2.
\label{eq:all-candidate-regression}
\end{equation}
The identity follows from $\sum_{i<i'}(u_i-u_{i'})^2=n\sum_i(u_i-\bar u)^2$ and needs one likelihood per candidate. Nonuniform pair measures require their original weights. Verify values and gradients against explicit pairs. Both full-parameter pilot arms share initialization, targets and predeclared learning rate, token budget and stopping rule. Report train/dev fit, $\KL(p^\star\Vert\widetilde p_\theta)$, lengths and fresh wins; keep these pilots separate from the $10$k-prompt, $1{,}250$-update runs.

\paragraph{Target separation and objective conflict.}
Recompute all entries in Table~\ref{tab:target-signal} on this same development panel and pair measure. Target-difference RMS is $\sqrt{\E[(\widehat h_a^\star-\widehat h_b^\star)^2]}$. Let $e_a=h_a-\widehat h_a^\star$; fit-error RMS is $\sqrt{\E[e_a^2]}$ and error-difference RMS is $\sqrt{\E[(e_a-e_b)^2]}$. The identity $h_a-h_b=(\widehat h_a^\star-\widehat h_b^\star)+(e_a-e_b)$ distinguishes shared fitting error from error that changes the method contrast. Compare their scales without interpreting their ratio as a statistical power calculation or proof of signal loss. Report cross-objective winner disagreement, non-tie denominators and rank correlations. These measure conflict, not within-objective cycles; independent-judge agreement assesses teacher transfer.

Measured on that panel, the two terms are far apart in scale. The \NBPO{} and utilitarian targets
differ by RMS $0.179$ and the \NBPO{} and fixed-reference targets by $0.418$, against a target RMS
of $5.38$ and mean total variations of $0.021$ and $0.024$ between the target distributions
themselves. The fitted arms' errors are RMS $4.21$ for \NBPO{} and $4.28$--$4.30$ for the
controls, so the regression leaves most of the target unexplained at this budget. Their error
\emph{difference} is RMS $1.18$ and $1.23$, which is $6.6$ and $2.9$ times the corresponding
target difference, and the difference between the two fitted policies is RMS $1.18$ and $1.20$ --
numerically the error difference rather than the target difference. We report these as scales and
draw no power calculation from their ratio. Read with the independent panel, where the same three
targets differ by at most $0.0018$ in judged win rate, the two measurements say the same thing
from different directions: what distinguishes these aggregation rules is small both in the
numbers the regression must reproduce and in the quality an independent evaluator can detect.
